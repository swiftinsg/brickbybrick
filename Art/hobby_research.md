# Teen hobbies and schoolwork: research notes

Research for **focushouse**, a study planner for students aged 13–18. The aim was to ground the furniture catalogue in what teenagers actually do, using published surveys and national statistics rather than assumptions.

## How to read this

There is no single global survey that ranks teen hobbies across the US, UK, Singapore and Australia. The ranking below combines the closest available measures from several large studies. Percentages describe different age bands and different questions, so they are **not** directly comparable with each other. The order is a synthesis: broadly-used activities first, then popular but narrower ones.

All sources below were fetched and read while preparing this document. Figures are quoted as the source reports them.

### Notes on the ranking

- **Watching and social media** are separated because they support different furniture: a screen/TV setup versus a phone/content-creation setup.
- **Gaming** is the clearest single "hobby identity" in the US data: four in ten teens call themselves gamers, and 62% of teen boys do.
- **Music** is included on time-spent and wellbeing grounds; the IFPI study covers 16–64s, so it is not teen-only.
- **Sport** is measured as organised participation, so casual walking, cycling or PE is not counted. The true share doing some physical activity is higher.
- **Reading** is included even though enjoyment is at a record low, because a third of young people still read for pleasure and school reading is universal.
- **Fashion** is supported by spending data rather than a "do you enjoy fashion" question.
- **Art** uses a broad arts-engagement measure that includes reading and cinema attendance, so 79% overstates dedicated drawing or craft. The activity list is the stronger evidence that drawing, crafts and photography are common.

## Schoolwork categories

Four categories were chosen for the planner. They are design categories based on the sources below and on the kinds of work set in lower-secondary schooling; they are not a ranked survey.

1. **Homework and assignments** — day-to-day tasks set after class. In Singapore, 13–19-year-olds spent almost three hours a day on screens for education, the largest single screen-time category. ([CNA–IPS survey](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/singapore/screen-time-devices-survey-teens-spend-daily-stress-4908281))
2. **Exam revision** — repeated, recall-heavy study. The same survey found teens spend nearly 8.5 hours a day on devices in total, with education the biggest use; revision is a large part of that load. ([CNA–IPS survey](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/singapore/screen-time-devices-survey-teens-spend-daily-stress-4908281))
3. **Essays and writing** — longer-form writing and coursework. The UK arts survey separately lists "written stories, plays, or poetry" as a common creative activity, showing writing is both schoolwork and leisure. ([UK Government, arts report](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/community-and-engagement-survey-202526-arts/community-and-engagement-survey-202526-arts-report))
4. **Group projects and presentations** — collaborative work and speaking tasks. Pew found that 72% of teen gamers play with others and 41% say gaming has helped them work with others, which reflects how central group work and collaboration are to teen life. ([Pew, *Teens and Video Games Today*](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/05/09/teens-and-video-games-today/))

## Design implications for focushouse

- The room should support **both consumption and creation**: a screen corner, a gaming corner, a music corner, and a making corner.
- Reading and writing are quieter, smaller-scale activities; their furniture should feel like a nook rather than a workstation.
- Sport and fashion need **storage and display**, not just equipment: a rail, a mirror, a mat roll, a treadmill.
- Schoolwork furniture should cover **capture, recall and output**: caddies and cards for homework, boards for revision, a typewriter/printer for writing, a pinboard/projector for group work.
- Because students earn up to 100 bricks a day, starters should be reachable in a day or two (10–60), while upgrades should feel like a week-long goal (30–180).
